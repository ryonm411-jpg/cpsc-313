Disks From Scratch

A spinning disk is a machine with moving parts. Every number in a disk question comes from the arm moving, the platter turning, or the data passing under the head.

Your win for today: compute the average time to read one sector (R4.7), and say what happens to each part when one setting changes (4.6). About 20 minutes.

1 · What's inside

WordMeaning
PlatterThe spinning circular surface that holds the data.
TrackOne ring on the platter.
SectorOne slice of a track, usually 512 bytes. The disk reads and writes whole sectors and never a single byte.
Head / armThe head reads the data passing under it. The arm moves it to the right track.
RPMRotations per minute: how fast the platter spins.

2 · Why disks aren't used like memory

Memory answers in about 100 ns. A disk takes about 10 ms, which is roughly 100,000 times slower, because it has to move metal. Two things follow from that:

  1. Disks are block devices. You can't load one byte from a disk. The OS asks for whole blocks (one or more sectors) and copies them into memory. Programs never point directly at disk data.
  2. Most of the cost is getting there, not reading. Once the head is in place, data streams quickly, so it pays to read big, contiguous chunks and to cache recently used blocks in memory.

Why can't a program do movq (disk address), %rax?

  1. Disks store data in little-endian order
  2. Disk addresses are always longer than 64 bits
  3. Disks transfer whole blocks, not single bytes
  4. The CPU is not allowed to read files

A disk is a block device: the OS reads whole sectors/blocks into memory, and only then can the program use load instructions on the copy.

3 · The three parts of an access

The formula access time = seek + rotational latency + transfer
  1. Seek: move the arm to the right track. The question gives you the average.
  2. Rotational latency: wait for the sector to spin under the head. On average that's half a rotation.
  3. Transfer: the sector passes under the head. That's one rotation ÷ sectors per track.

The only conversion you need: one rotation = 60,000 / RPM milliseconds, because a minute is 60,000 ms.

Worked example: R4.7

Seek 21 ms, 7200 RPM, 16 sectors per track.

  1. One rotation = 60,000 / 7200 = 8.3333 ms.
  2. Rotational latency = half = 4.1667 ms.
  3. Transfer = 8.3333 / 16 = 0.5208 ms.
  4. Total = 21 + 4.1667 + 0.5208 = 25.6875 ≈ 25.69 ms.
TrapRound only at the end. Rounding 4.17 + 0.52 early gives 25.69 here by luck, but on other numbers it lands 0.01 off, and that's outside PrairieLearn's tolerance.

A disk spins at 4000 RPM. What is its average rotational latency, in ms?

One rotation = 60,000 / 4000 = 15 ms. On average you wait half of it: 7.5 ms. If you typed 15, you forgot the "half".

Seek is the arm and has nothing to do with spinning. Rotation and transfer both scale with the rotation time. Throughput means bytes per second: the same bytes over a longer time is lower throughput.

Your 4.6 attempt You answered "insufficient information / smaller / the same" for a drive that spins more slowly. Using the table: throughput will be lower, seek time will be the same, and sector transfer time will be larger. "Insufficient information" is only right when the question changes two things that pull in opposite directions.

Drive 2 is identical to drive 1 but has a faster arm. Its rotational latency will…

  1. be smaller than drive 1
  2. be the same as drive 1
  3. be larger than drive 1
  4. be impossible to tell

Rotational latency depends only on RPM. The arm affects seek only.

Drive 2 spins faster and packs fewer sectors per track. The transfer time for one sector will…

  1. be impossible to tell
  2. be smaller than drive 1
  3. be larger than drive 1
  4. be the same as drive 1

Faster spin shrinks the rotation, but fewer sectors make each one a bigger slice of it. Without numbers the two effects can't be compared. This is the case where "insufficient information" is right.

For a small random read, which part usually dominates?

  1. Transfer time
  2. Moving bytes over the bus
  3. The CPU's cache
  4. Seek plus rotation

In R4.7, 25.17 of the 25.69 ms is positioning. That's why file systems try to place a file's blocks next to each other.
